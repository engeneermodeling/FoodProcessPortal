# Lecture 21. Size Reduction of Food Materials

## Learning Outcomes

After this lecture you will be able to explain why food materials are milled, distinguish compression, impact, attrition and cutting, interpret particle-size distributions, select equipment and identify quality and safety risks.

---

## 1. Purpose

Size reduction increases surface area and helps mixing, extraction, drying, cooking, fermentation and forming. It can also damage quality by heating aromatic materials, accelerating fat oxidation, creating dust or destroying desired texture.

Examples include flour milling, spices, meat grinding, chocolate and nut pastes, and dry ingredient blends.

---

## 2. Fracture Mechanisms

Food materials may be reduced by compression, impact, attrition or cutting. Real machines often combine several mechanisms.

## 3. Energy and Material Behaviour

Only part of the input energy creates new surface. Much becomes heat, noise, vibration and deformation. Dry brittle materials are easier to mill than wet, fatty, fibrous or sticky materials.

## 4. Particle-Size Distribution

Milling produces a distribution, not a single particle size.

![Particle-size distribution](/images/lectures/mechanical/particle-size-distribution.svg)

Important values include `d10`, `d50`, `d90`, dust fraction, oversize fraction and distribution uniformity. Particle size affects flavour release, dissolution, suspension stability, mouthfeel and packaging behaviour.

---

## 5. Equipment Selection

Equipment choice depends on material, target size, throughput, heat sensitivity, moisture, fat content, hygiene and dust risk.

![Mill selection for food materials](/images/lectures/mechanical/mill-selection-food.svg)

Roller mills suit grains and controlled deformation. Hammer mills suit dry brittle materials. Pin or disc mills suit fine dry grinding. Knife grinders suit meat, vegetables, cheese and fibrous materials. Ball or roll refiners suit chocolate masses and pastes. Cryogenic grinding may help aromatic or fatty products.
