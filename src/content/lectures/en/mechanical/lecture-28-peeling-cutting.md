# Lecture 28. Peeling, Cutting and Slicing of Food Products

## Learning Outcomes

After this lecture you will be able to distinguish size reduction from controlled cutting, describe peeling and dicing principles, identify factors affecting cut quality and recognize hygienic risks in cutting machines.

---

## 1. Controlled Cutting

Cutting gives a product a defined shape, size and surface. French fries require uniform strips, frozen vegetables need stable dices, cheese needs even slices and meat products may need coarse chopping or fine emulsions.

![Cutting geometries](/images/lectures/mechanical/cutting-geometries.svg)

Cut quality affects appearance, heat transfer, juice losses, oxidation, sticking, freezing or drying uniformity, dosing and packaging.

---

## 2. Peeling

Peeling removes an unwanted outer layer with minimum loss of edible material. Methods include abrasive peeling, knife peeling, steam peeling, caustic peeling followed by washing, flame peeling and manual trimming.

---

## 3. Slicing and Dicing

Dicing is usually a sequence: slice, strip and crosscut.

![Dicing principle](/images/lectures/mechanical/dicing-principle.svg)

Stable cutting requires uniform feeding, product holding, sharp knives, correct speed, suitable temperature and cleaning to avoid buildup.

---

## 4. Bowl Cutting and Emulsifying

Bowl cutters prepare meat, fish, vegetable, cheese and plant-based masses. High-speed knives can create coarse fillings, fine emulsions or pastes while mixing and changing temperature and viscosity.

---

## 5. Quality Factors

![Cutting quality factors](/images/lectures/mechanical/cutting-quality-factors.svg)

Sharp knives reduce compression and juice loss. Product temperature controls firmness. Uniform feed is as important as the knife itself. Poor settings cause ragged edges, crumbs, deformation, sticking, overheating or metal risk from worn knives.
