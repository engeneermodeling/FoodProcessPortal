# Lecture 19. Absorption, Adsorption and Stream Purification in Food Processing

## Learning Outcomes

After this lecture you will be able to:

- distinguish absorption from adsorption
- describe wet scrubbers and activated-carbon beds
- select a purification approach for a gas or liquid stream
- explain the role of equilibrium, contact area and residence time
- understand adsorber breakthrough
- connect the theory with odours, syrups, water, beverages and refrigeration gases

---

## 1. Two Related Operations

Absorption transfers a component into a liquid. A gas stream containing odour compounds, ammonia or acidic gases contacts water, acid, alkali or another scrubbing liquid, and part of the contaminant dissolves or reacts.

Adsorption retains molecules on the surface of a solid. In food processing, activated carbon is widely used to remove colour, taste, odours and trace organics from air, water, syrups and beverages.

## 2. Wet Scrubbing

In a scrubber, gas and liquid contact each other counter-currently, co-currently or cross-currently. The apparatus creates a large interfacial area using droplets, liquid films, packing or trays.

![Absorption scrubber](/images/lectures/mass-transfer/absorption-scrubber.svg)

Typical duties include ammonia absorption, odour reduction, gas cooling, removal of acidic or alkaline contaminants, and pre-treatment before final polishing.

The scrubbing liquid must match the impurity. Water is not always enough; chemical neutralisation or a special solvent may be required.

---

## 3. Driving Force

Absorption proceeds while the actual gas concentration differs from the equilibrium concentration:

```text
N_A = k_G * A * (y_A - y_A*)
```

where `N_A` is transfer rate, `k_G` is a gas-side mass-transfer coefficient, `A` is contact area, `y_A` is the actual gas concentration, and `y_A*` is the equilibrium value.

Efficiency increases with contact area, turbulence, residence time and a liquid that has a strong affinity for the target component.

---

## 4. Adsorption on Solids

In an adsorber, the stream passes through a porous solid. The most familiar food-processing example is activated carbon, but zeolites, ion-exchange resins and polymeric sorbents are also used.

![Fixed-bed adsorber](/images/lectures/mass-transfer/adsorption-bed.svg)

Adsorption is useful for odour control, syrup and juice decolourisation, water polishing, trace-organic removal and flavour stabilisation.

---

## 5. Breakthrough

A sorbent bed has finite capacity. The mass-transfer zone moves through the bed. When the contaminant appears at the outlet above the allowable level, breakthrough has occurred.

Important operating variables are inlet concentration, flow rate, temperature, humidity or liquid composition, bed depth, particle size, outlet specification and regeneration or replacement strategy.

---

## 6. Comparison

| Feature | Absorption | Adsorption |
|---|---|---|
| Capturing phase | liquid | solid surface |
| Typical equipment | scrubber, packed column | activated-carbon filter, fixed bed |
| Typical impurities | NH3, H2S, SO2, CO2, odours | odours, colour bodies, VOCs, trace organics |
| Limitation | spent scrubbing liquid | sorbent saturation and breakthrough |
| Monitoring | pH, liquid flow, pressure drop, outlet concentration | outlet concentration, pressure drop, bed life |

---

## 7. Food Examples

Sugar syrups may be treated with powdered or granular activated carbon and then filtered. Frying or drying exhaust may require aerosol removal, cooling, scrubbing and adsorption. Ammonia refrigeration systems may use water or acidic emergency scrubbers. Beverage and process-water lines may use activated carbon for odour and organic-trace control.
