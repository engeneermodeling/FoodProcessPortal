# Lecture 32. Energy Saving and Resource Efficiency in Food Processes

## What You Will Learn

After this lecture you will be able to:

- identify the main resource losses in a food processing line
- explain the role of heat recovery in pasteurization, cooling and evaporation
- assess the impact of pumps, fans, compressors and refrigeration systems
- understand how automation reduces water, steam, refrigeration and CIP chemicals
- distinguish useful savings from actions that harm safety or quality
- use simple indicators such as kWh/t, water/t, steam/t and product loss

---

## 1. Resource Efficiency Is More Than Electricity

Food processing consumes many resources: steam heats product, refrigeration removes heat, water cleans equipment, compressed air moves valves, pumps create flow, and packaging lines may lose product through unstable filling.

Resource efficiency therefore means more than switching lights off. It starts with understanding where losses occur.

![Resource loss map](/images/lectures/automation-control/resource-loss-map.svg)

---

## 2. Indicators Worth Tracking

It is useful to compare specific rather than absolute consumption:

- kWh per tonne of product
- kg steam per tonne of product
- m3 water per tonne of product
- compressed air per hour or batch
- detergent per CIP cycle
- percentage product loss
- downtime caused by cleaning or changeover

Without these indicators it is hard to know whether the line became more efficient or simply produced less today.

---

## 3. Heat and Recovery

Large opportunities often come from heat. If a hot product must be cooled and a cold stream must be heated, heat can be transferred between them through a heat exchanger. This is the principle of regenerative heating in milk, juice, beverage and other liquid food processes.

![Heat recovery cascade](/images/lectures/automation-control/heat-recovery-cascade.svg)

Heat recovery reduces both steam demand for heating and refrigeration demand for cooling. It requires clean heat-transfer surfaces, correct flow distribution, pressure control and hygienic safety.

---

## 4. Steam, Condensate and Insulation

Steam systems lose resources through leaks, failed steam traps, poor condensate return, weak insulation, excessive pressure, heating equipment during idle time and fouled heat exchangers.

Condensate already contains heat and treated water, so returning it often gives a clear saving. Insulating pipes, tanks and vessels reduces losses and improves workplace safety.

---

## 5. Refrigeration Systems

Refrigeration is more expensive than it may appear. Compressor power depends on evaporation and condensation temperatures, heat-exchanger cleanliness, fan operation, refrigerant charge and load.

Typical actions include avoiding unnecessary overcooling, keeping condensers and evaporators clean, reducing heat ingress through doors and uninsulated sections, recovering cold where possible and avoiding simultaneous heating and cooling of the same stream.

---

## 6. Pumps, Fans and Compressed Air

Pumps and fans often run for long periods, so small improvements can matter. Variable-speed drives allow speed reduction instead of throttling with a valve. The pump still has to operate in an acceptable part of its curve, without cavitation or unstable flow.

Compressed air is an expensive utility. Leaks, excessive pressure and using air where a mechanical or electric drive would be enough can quickly increase consumption.

---

## 7. CIP and Water

CIP consumes water, heat, chemicals and time. Savings must not reduce cleanliness. Optimization can come from conductivity-based phase switching, reuse of suitable solutions, removal of unnecessary rinses, measurement of actual temperature and flow, better grouping of circuits and hygienic design that avoids hard-to-clean dead zones.

The best CIP saving often starts not with a shorter program, but with better cleanability.

---

## 8. Monitoring and Improvement Loop

Resource efficiency works like a control loop: measure, compare, act, verify.

![Energy monitoring loop](/images/lectures/automation-control/energy-monitoring-loop.svg)

Plants need more than a monthly invoice. Trends by line, batch, shift and operating mode reveal problems such as a CIP cycle using more water, a compressed-air leak, or a heat exchanger requiring more steam after fouling.

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

- counting only electricity and ignoring steam, refrigeration, water and product
- shortening CIP without cleanliness verification
- reducing process temperature without safety analysis
- installing a variable-speed drive without changing control logic
- not measuring specific consumption per unit product
- ignoring start-up, shutdown and changeover product losses
- blaming automation while heat exchangers are fouled
