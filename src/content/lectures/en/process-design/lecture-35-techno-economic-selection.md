# Lecture 35. Techno-Economic Justification of Equipment Selection

## What You Will Learn

After this lecture you will be able to distinguish CAPEX, OPEX and total cost of ownership, explain why the cheapest machine is not always the best option, estimate payback, include product losses, downtime, CIP and service in the decision, and build a simple comparison matrix.

---

## 1. Why Engineers Need Economics

An engineering solution must be technically correct and suitable for the plant. It has to be affordable, reliable, safe, resource-efficient and maintainable.

In food production, economics depends strongly on product quality, raw-material losses, steam, refrigeration, water, detergents, downtime and product returns. Equipment selection cannot be reduced to the purchase price in a quotation.

---

## 2. CAPEX, OPEX and TCO

CAPEX includes equipment purchase, transport, installation, connections, commissioning, training and sometimes building modifications. OPEX includes electricity, steam, water, refrigeration, compressed air, chemicals, labour, spare parts, service, downtime, waste and product losses. TCO is the total cost of ownership over the operating life.

![CAPEX, OPEX and TCO](/images/lectures/process-design/capex-opex-tco.svg)

A cheaper machine may consume more steam, take longer to clean and stop more often. Its TCO can therefore be higher.

---

## 3. What to Calculate

For food line comparison, estimate investment cost, capacity, real operating time, energy use, water and CIP use, product loss during start-up, shutdown and changeover, reject rate, service cost, cleaning effort, utility requirements and impact on product quality and safety.

Some factors are hard to calculate exactly, but they should not be ignored. An honest assumption is better than pretending the cost is zero.

---

## 4. Payback

Simple payback shows how many years of savings are needed to return the additional investment.

![Payback and sensitivity](/images/lectures/process-design/payback-sensitivity.svg)

A more expensive heat exchanger may recover more heat. If it saves steam and refrigeration every year, the additional CAPEX may return quickly. Payback must be calculated using the real operating schedule, real energy price and real throughput.

---

## 5. Sensitivity

A decision can be attractive under one set of assumptions and weak under another. Check what happens if energy prices rise, annual operating hours are lower, product-loss reduction is only half of expected, service is more expensive or equipment causes additional downtime.

Sensitivity shows how robust the decision is.

---

## 6. Decision Matrix

Not every criterion can be reduced to money. A decision matrix helps in course projects.

![Equipment decision matrix](/images/lectures/process-design/decision-matrix.svg)

Criteria may include product quality, energy use, water use, hygienic design, CIP, capacity, flexibility, maintenance, service availability, price and risk. Weight the criteria, score the options and explain the result. The matrix does not replace engineering judgement, but makes the argument clearer.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

- comparing only purchase price
- ignoring installation and connections
- forgetting water, steam, refrigeration and CIP
- not counting product losses
- ignoring downtime and changeovers
- not considering service availability
- giving payback without showing assumptions
- selecting equipment that is unnecessarily complex
- saving money by sacrificing hygienic design

---

## 8. Course-Project Conclusion

A good conclusion answers three questions: which option was selected, why it is technically suitable and why it is economically and operationally justified.

For example: the selected option has higher CAPEX, but lower specific steam use, shorter CIP, lower start-up losses and better maintenance access. Under the expected operating mode, the additional investment is offset by lower OPEX.
