# Lecture 43. Piping Systems of Food Processing Lines

## What You Will Learn

After this lecture you will be able to explain how pipe diameter is selected, why velocity affects pressure drop and CIP, how stagnant zones are avoided, why slopes and drains are needed, and how piping connects pumps, valves and equipment.

---

## 1. A Pipe Is Part of the Equipment System

In a food line, a pipe is not a neutral connection. It affects hydraulic resistance, residence time, start-up and shutdown losses, cleanability, stagnation risk, heating or cooling of product and maintenance access.

If equipment is selected correctly but the piping is poor, the whole line may be unstable: the pump will not deliver flow, CIP will not clean a branch, product will remain in pockets and sensors may not represent the real product condition.

![Piping design factors](/images/lectures/process-design/piping-design-factors.svg)

---

## 2. Flow Rate, Velocity and Diameter

The basic relationship is:

**Q = v · A**

For a given flow rate, a smaller diameter gives higher velocity and higher pressure drop. A larger diameter lowers velocity but increases product volume in the line, switching losses, cost and sometimes makes CIP less effective.

Diameter should therefore not be oversized blindly. Production mode, CIP mode, pump, viscosity, acceptable product velocity and pressure drop along the longest route must be checked.

---

## 3. Pressure Drop

Pressure losses occur in straight pipes, bends, tees, reducers, valves, filters, heat exchangers and equipment. They increase with velocity and depend on viscosity, density, roughness, flow regime and geometry.

For a course project, it is useful to show product flow rate, approximate pipe velocity, main line length, number of local resistances, total head loss and whether the pump can provide the required flow.

This turns a scheme from a nice picture into an engineering calculation.

---

## 4. Drainability and Slopes

Food piping should drain after production and cleaning. If pockets remain, product, water or cleaning solution collects there.

![Self-draining pipe slope](/images/lectures/process-design/self-draining-pipe-slope.svg)

Food lines are often installed with a slight slope toward drains. The slope must not be lost through poor supports, sagging, installation errors or thermal expansion.

Drains must be accessible, safe and clear to the operator. Otherwise product remains in the line after shutdown.

---

## 5. Dead Legs and Branches

A dead leg is a section with weak or no flow. Product stagnates there, heats or cools differently, cleans poorly and may create contamination risk.

![Dead legs in piping](/images/lectures/process-design/dead-leg-rule.svg)

Risk increases with long blind branches, poor tees, inactive outlets, badly installed sensors, sampling points and manual valves. Assessment should consider not only branch length but also product, temperature, frequency of use, CIP regime and drainability.

---

## 6. Materials, Connections and Surface

Food-contact piping is usually stainless steel. Important factors include steel grade, weld quality, internal surface, absence of crevices, correct seals and compatibility with product and cleaning chemicals.

Demountable joints are needed for service or removal. But each joint is a possible error point: wrong gasket, misalignment, crevice, poor tightening or unsuitable material.

For permanent sections, high-quality welding with internal weld control is often the better solution.

---

## 7. Supports, Access and Thermal Expansion

Piping has weight, vibration and thermal expansion. Supports should hold the line without transferring harmful stress to equipment, pumps or valves.

Consider access to valves and joints, inspection and repair, thermal expansion, sagging prevention, mechanical protection and location above walkways or working areas.

Good piping does not obstruct the operator and does not require awkward maintenance.

---

## 8. Piping and CIP

CIP mode often differs from production mode. Cleaning needs sufficient velocity, turbulence, temperature, concentration and contact time. A pipe selected only for slow viscous product may be difficult to clean without a separate solution.

The scheme should show where cleaning solution enters, how it returns, which valves switch the mode, where drains are located, whether branches are cleaned and how final rinse completion is checked.

A pipe that cannot be cleaned is not a finished engineering solution.

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

- choosing diameter only “with reserve”
- ignoring pressure drop in valves and fittings
- ignoring product viscosity
- installing horizontal sections without drains
- leaving blind branches
- placing sensors in stagnant locations
- forgetting thermal expansion
- making valves inaccessible
- not checking the CIP route
