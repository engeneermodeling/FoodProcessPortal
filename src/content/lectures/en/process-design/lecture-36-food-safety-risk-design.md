# Lecture 36. Food Process Safety and Risk in Line Design

## What You Will Learn

After this lecture you will be able to name the main food safety hazards, distinguish GHP/PRP, OPRP and CCP, identify risks on a process scheme, consider zoning, cross-contamination and allergens, and understand why CCP monitoring must be fast and documented.

---

## 1. Safety Starts Before Start-Up

Food safety cannot be added at the end by testing final product. It is built into raw-material control, product routing, temperature regimes, hygienic design, CIP, zoning, automation, packaging and documentation.

Engineers must see where equipment or layout can create risk: dead zones, wrong valves, insufficient temperature, weak cooling, missing drainage, crossing of raw and ready-to-eat zones or inability to verify a critical parameter.

![Food line hazard map](/images/lectures/process-design/hazard-map-food-line.svg)

---

## 2. Hazard Groups

HACCP usually considers biological hazards, chemical hazards, physical hazards and allergens. Each hazard should be assessed by likelihood, severity and available control measures.

---

## 3. GHP/PRP, OPRP and CCP

Not every control point is a critical control point. Good hygiene practices and prerequisite programs provide the foundation: sanitation, personal hygiene, water, pest control, training, equipment maintenance, calibration and storage control.

An OPRP manages a significant risk during operation. A CCP is a step where control is essential to eliminate a hazard or reduce it to an acceptable level.

![HACCP control points](/images/lectures/process-design/haccp-control-points.svg)

Pasteurization with time-temperature control is a typical CCP. A metal detector or sieve may be an OPRP or CCP depending on the product and the food safety management system. General room sanitation is usually PRP.

---

## 4. Risks on the Process Scheme

When reading a scheme, look for where product reaches a safe treatment, where it can be recontaminated, where product is open, how raw and ready zones are separated, where rejected product goes, how CIP is performed, where drains are located, where critical sensors are installed and whether records and interlocks exist.

If a point is critical, it is not enough to label it. The scheme and procedure must show how it is controlled and what happens when it deviates.

---

## 5. Zoning and Cross-Contamination

One of the most common risks is contamination moving from raw areas to clean or ready-to-eat areas.

![Zoning and cross-contamination](/images/lectures/process-design/zoning-cross-contamination.svg)

Separate raw and finished product flows, personnel routes, tools, packaging, waste, airflows and drains. Areas after pasteurization, cooking, washing or any risk-reducing step require special care.

---

## 6. Monitoring, Records and Corrective Actions

CCPs require critical limits, monitoring, corrective actions, verification and records. Monitoring must be fast enough to prevent product made during a deviation from moving forward without a decision.

Examples: product is diverted when pasteurization temperature is below the limit; product since the last successful check is isolated if the metal detector fails a test piece; a line is not released after CIP if conductivity shows chemical residues.

Without records, the plant cannot prove that the process was under control.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

- calling every sensor or inspection a CCP
- confusing safety with quality
- forgetting recontamination after heat treatment
- not showing raw and ready zones on the layout
- ignoring allergens during product changeover
- relying on final product testing instead of process control
- having no corrective action for deviations
- not storing critical parameter records
