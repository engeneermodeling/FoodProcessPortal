# Lecture 40. Water, Wastewater, Waste and Sustainability of Food Lines

## What You Will Learn

After this lecture you will be able to explain where a food line consumes water and creates wastewater, build a simple water balance, evaluate product losses, propose a hierarchy for side streams and include resource efficiency in a course project.

---

## 1. Sustainability Starts With the Line Layout

Environmental performance is not only waste sorting. It is built into equipment choice, pipe length, CIP design, heat recovery, start-up and shutdown logic, dosing accuracy, batch planning and the possibility of using side streams.

If a line drains poorly, product goes to wastewater. If CIP is too long, water, heat and chemicals are wasted. If a heat exchanger fouls quickly, energy use rises. Resource efficiency is therefore an engineering task, not a decorative statement.

---

## 2. Water Balance

A water balance shows how much water enters the process, where it is used and where it leaves.

![Water balance of a food line](/images/lectures/process-design/water-balance-food-line.svg)

For a food line, consider water as ingredient, cleaning water, CIP, cooling, steam and condensate, rinsing, sanitation, leaks, product-containing wastewater and detergent-containing wastewater.

A useful indicator is **specific water consumption = m3 of water / t of finished product**.

---

## 3. Wastewater Is Not Just Water

Food wastewater may contain organic matter, fats, proteins, sugars, starch, salts, detergents, acids, alkalis, suspended solids and temperature variation. The load on treatment systems often grows because product enters drains, not only because water volume is high.

Typical causes include long start-ups and shutdowns, poor draining, tank overflows, rinsing product to drain before CIP, wrong valve sequence, no collection of first and last product, and emergency dumping without root-cause analysis.

Losses should be reduced at the source before treatment capacity is increased.

---

## 4. Resource Loss Map

To reduce losses, the team must see them.

![Resource loss map](/images/lectures/process-design/resource-loss-map.svg)

For each loss, record where it occurs, when, how much, why, who owns it, how it is measured and what can be done. Start-up losses in a pasteurizer, for example, may be reduced by better divert logic, smaller pipe volume after the balance tank or better batch sequencing.

---

## 5. CIP and Resource Efficiency

CIP must be safe and verified. But “more water and longer time” does not always mean better cleaning. Engineers optimize product displacement, separation of concentrated and diluted wastewater, temperature, concentration, time, reuse of cleaning solutions, final rinse conductivity, air blowing or pigging for viscous products and production scheduling to reduce the number of washes.

CIP optimization must never reduce food safety. It has to be confirmed by cleanliness checks.

---

## 6. Side Streams and Use Hierarchy

Not everything that is not the main product should immediately become waste.

![Food use and waste hierarchy](/images/lectures/process-design/food-use-waste-hierarchy.svg)

Priorities are: prevent the loss, use it as food or ingredient when safe, use it as feed or technical product, process it industrially, recover energy or compost, and dispose only when better options are impossible.

In a course project, it is enough to describe two or three main side streams and propose realistic uses.

---

## 7. Packaging and Finished Product Losses

Packaging affects sustainability twice: it creates waste and it can reduce food loss by protecting the product. Therefore, packaging should not be judged only by material weight.

Important line factors include dosing accuracy, label rejects, poor sealing, damaged containers, changeover losses and returns caused by marking errors. Sometimes a small sealing improvement reduces product loss more than saving a few grams of packaging material.

---

## 8. What to Add to a Course Project

A short “resource efficiency” block can include specific water consumption, main wastewater sources, possible product losses, CIP organization, heat recovery, side-stream use and operating indicators to monitor.

This shows that the student sees the line as a real production object, not just a set of machines.
