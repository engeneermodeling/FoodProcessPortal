# Laboratory Work №21. Studying the Kinetics of the Convective Drying Process of Food Materials

## Theoretical Background

Drying is a heat and mass transfer process of removing moisture from materials (dehydrating them) by supplying heat to them (by convection, thermal conduction, radiation, or a certain combined method).

In particular, in an air convective dryer, which is usually a chamber-type construction, moving atmospheric air (the drying agent), heated in a heater to a technologically permissible temperature, contacts the material placed on transport or supporting means. Due to the existing temperature difference between the material's surface and the heated air, heat exchange occurs between them, and, as a result, evaporation of free moisture from the material's surface begins and continues for some time. The difference in partial pressures of water vapor in the boundary vapor-air layer of the material and in the external air stream, which is the driving force of the drying process, ensures the removal of physically and mechanically bound moisture and a constant rate of this process, i.e., a constant mass of evaporated moisture per unit time from a unit surface of the material. This stage of dehydrating the material is called the first drying period, or the constant drying rate period, and the process itself — the phenomenon of moisture (its mass) moving from the material's surface into the air stream — is called external diffusion. Therefore, the first drying period is also called the external diffusion period.

After the removal of free moisture (wetting moisture), the process of moisture movement from the inner layers to the surface of the material begins, due to the difference in moisture concentration in the deep and already partially dehydrated outer layers, i.e., the process of internal diffusion begins and develops, but now not of physically and mechanically bound moisture, but of so-called physically and chemically bound moisture (adsorptively and osmotically bound moisture, structural moisture, etc.).

When the mass of moisture arriving from the inner layers to the surface of the material becomes less than the mass of moisture that can evaporate from the surface (for steady process conditions), the drying rate begins to decrease.

The beginning of the decrease in drying rate is characterized by the so-called first critical moisture content of the material Wcr1, and the subsequent period of removing physically and chemically bound moisture is called the second period, or the falling drying rate period (the internal moisture diffusion period). This period can continue until the material reaches an equilibrium Wcr2, or the so-called second critical moisture content. When Wcr2 is reached, the amount of moisture removed from the material into the surrounding medium (air) and the amount of moisture returning from it into the material (per unit time) become nearly equal. In this case, the drying process becomes impossible and is stopped.

If the reached Wcr2 is greater than the technologically required or ordered final moisture content of the material W2, the driving forces of external ΔP and internal ΔC moisture diffusion are usually restored by increasing the temperature of the drying agent (if technologically permissible), reducing its moisture content (conditioning), or reducing the overall pressure in the drying chamber.

It should be noted that the internal transfer of moisture in the material due to so-called thermodiffusion — the flow of moisture under the action of a temperature gradient, directed from the surface or layer of material with a higher temperature to the inner layers with a lower temperature — can significantly affect the rate of the drying process. Under conditions of convective drying, this phenomenon of thermal moisture conductivity can be influential only in the second period — in the period of internal diffusion. The negative effect of thermodiffusion can be prevented by vigorously heating the entire thickness of the material to a constant temperature before the end of the first period, or during the second period, by periodically stopping the supply of drying agent, thereby cooling the surface of the material; as a result, the direction of the thermal gradient changes. In this case, the directions of moisture movement caused by thermodiffusion and concentration diffusion coincide, which causes an increase in the flow of moisture from the inner to the outer layers of the material, i.e., an increase in the drying rate.

The nature of the change in moisture content Wi of the material during its drying is illustrated by the so-called drying curve, whose graph is plotted from experimental data. From fig. 21.1 it can be seen that after a fairly short time τ0 — the period of heating the material's surface to the wet-bulb temperature — the steady period of external diffusion begins (the first period), lasting τ1. During this period, the moisture content of the material decreases according to a linear law until the first critical moisture content Wcr1 is reached (the first critical point K1), after which the internal diffusion period begins (the second period): the moisture content of the material, over time τ2, decreases according to an exponential law and asymptotically reaches the equilibrium moisture content (the second critical moisture content Wcr2; the second critical point K2) or the required (technological) final moisture content W2, and the temperature of the material becomes approximately equal to the temperature of the drying agent.

The efficiency of each of the two drying periods, namely the process rate, is determined by the decrease in the material's moisture content dWi over an infinitesimally small time interval dτ, %/min, (i.e., at the i-th time of drying):

Ui = dWi/dτi. 	(21.1)

If in the first period the process rate is determined by the temperature and, to a certain extent, the velocity of motion (specific volumetric flow rate) of the drying agent in the drying chamber, then in the second period it depends on the amount and mass ratio of the different forms of physically and chemically bound moisture in the material and on the influence of thermodiffusion.

The rate of the drying process in each of the two periods is determined by one of the available methods — graphical differentiation of the drying curve. On the graph of the experimental drying curve (see fig. 21.1), tangent lines are drawn from various points on it, chosen arbitrarily (for example, with equal steps along the ordinate Wi), and the tangents of the angles of inclination of these tangent lines to the drying curve are determined, whose values constitute the values of the rates Ui of the process at the chosen points, i.e.,

Ui = dWi /dτi = tgαi , 	(21.2)

where i is the number of the chosen time point.

For the first period, the drying rate is determined by the formula

U1 = const = tgα1 = (WA – Wcr1)/τ1,    (21.3)

where WA is the moisture content of the material at the beginning of the first drying period, %.

If, on graph paper, the values of the material's moisture content Wi are plotted on the abscissa axis, and the corresponding drying rates (at previously chosen scales) on the ordinate axis, then, by connecting the intersection points of the corresponding abscissa and ordinate values, one obtains a graph of the change in drying process rate, or the so-called drying rate curve, Ui = dWi /dτi = f(Wi) (fig. 21.2). Such a representation of the drying rate curve makes it possible to analyze the physical meaning of the drying process, because the degree of curvature of the graph depends on the structure of the corresponding material, on the presence and ratio of moisture masses of various forms of bonding in the material.

Fig. 21.3, as an example, shows for the second period graphs of Ui = f(Wi) for materials of various structures: 3 — for most ceramic materials; 1 — for coarse-porous thin materials (cardboard, paper, etc.); 2, 5 — for capillary-porous and colloidal-capillary materials (bread, dough, sausage, beets, carrots, potatoes, etc.); 4 — for thin sheet materials (leather, fabric, etc.).

The drying duration, which characterizes the energy efficiency of the process, for the first period τ1, min, is determined by the formula

τ1 = (WA – Wcr1)/U1, 	(21.4)

where WA and Wcr1 are, respectively, the initial (in the first period) and the first critical moisture content of the material, %; U1 is the drying rate in the first period, %/min.

## Laboratory Setup

The drying setup (fig. 21.4) is a thermally insulated metal circulation loop 1, in which the air stream is moved by the blades of fan 2, passes sequentially through electric heater 3 and drying chamber 4, in which it contacts the material, supplying heat to it and removing evaporated moisture.

Next, from the drying chamber, the spent air again moves to the inlet of fan 2, which ensures the circulation of air in the dryer's circulation loop.

The material to be dried is placed in box-shaped mesh basket 5 (in the internal space of the drying chamber), connected via rod 6 to electric scale 7. The mesh basket 5 itself is removable, i.e., it can be disconnected from the rod and taken out of the drying chamber through a side hatch opening, closed by a movable glass shutter. Through this shutter, the state of the material can be observed during drying.

The flow rate and corresponding velocity of motion of the heated air in the drying chamber are regulated by damper 8 and monitored with an anemometer; the temperatures of the air before the heater t0, after the heater t1, and after the drying chamber t2 are monitored with thermometers 9, 10, 11. The specified (required) air temperature t1 at the entrance to the chamber is regulated by electric-contact thermometer 12, using relay block 13, connected to electric heating element 14 of heater 3.

## Procedure

1. We warm up the setup for 10–15 minutes. For this, we fix the specified temperature t1 on thermometer 12, open damper 8 to the specified degree, and sequentially turn on fan 2 and heater 3 (heating element 14).

2. We chop the material to be dried (potatoes, beets, carrots, etc.), with a mass of 100 g (50 g), into thin strips 2–4 mm thick.

3. We turn off heater 3 and fan 2, remove basket 5 from chamber 4, load 100 g (50 g) of the material's strips into it, and fasten it in the drying chamber on rod 6; we tightly close the drying chamber's glass shutter.

4. We sequentially turn on fan 2 and heater 3 and immediately record the start time of drying.

5. We record the change in the material's mass during drying (from the dial of scale 7) and enter it into table 21.3. For this, during the first 15 minutes of drying, we record the scale readings in table 21.3 every 2 minutes, and thereafter — every 5 minutes, and so on until the scale readings stop changing, i.e., until the material reaches the equilibrium moisture content Wcr2.

6. We sequentially turn off the power supply of heater 3 and fan 2, remove basket 5 from the chamber, and inspect the state of the dried material.
