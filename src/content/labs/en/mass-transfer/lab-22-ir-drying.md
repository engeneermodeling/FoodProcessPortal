# Laboratory Work №22. Studying the Kinetics of the Thermoradiation Drying Process of Food Products

## Theoretical Background

The term "infrared" comes from the Latin word infra, corresponding to the English word "below" or "beneath" (in German — unterhalb). There is a theory that the term "infrared" was introduced in 1869 by E. Becquerel, by analogy with the term "ultraviolet," proposed by Stokes in 1852. In German physical literature, the term "ultra-red" is used.

When heating and drying moist materials with infrared radiation, radiant energy is converted into heat. In this case, heat and mass transfer occur both outside the material — in the apparatus's working chamber — and inside the material.

As is known, during convective drying, air is the carrier of heat from the heater to the product, which is more energy-consuming than during thermoradiation drying, in which air does not perform the function of heat carrier, but only removes moisture.

The water molecules present in the product absorb infrared rays, i.e., energy is supplied directly to the water of the product, whereby a high efficiency is achieved. With this method of heat supply, there is no need to significantly raise the temperature during drying of the product, and the drying process can be carried out at a temperature of 40–60°C. In this case, the quality of the product is preserved to the maximum extent: cells are not ruptured, vitamins are preserved, sugar does not caramelize. In addition, low temperatures do not heat the drying equipment, i.e., there are no heat losses through the walls and ventilation. At the same time, infrared radiation at a temperature of 40–60 degrees makes it possible to destroy all microflora on the surface of the product, making the dried product practically sterile. The drawbacks of IR drying include the fact that the energy of IR radiation is absorbed mainly by the surface of the material being dried, and to prevent it from cracking and deforming, i.e., worsening the quality of the finished product, the amount of supplied energy has to be reduced. These same indicators are also worsened by the continuous supply of IR radiation energy, which creates a temperature gradient directed into the interior of the sliced pieces of product. This hinders heat and mass transfer, i.e., worsens the conditions for moisture movement from the inner to the outer layers. For food products, the penetration depth of infrared ray energy reaches 6–12 mm. A small part of the radiation energy penetrates to this depth, but the temperature of the layer located 6–7 mm from the surface of the material rises considerably more intensively than during heating by the convective method.

The infrared range includes rays with a wavelength from 0.77 to 340 µm. In the thermal treatment of materials, infrared rays with a wavelength of up to 5–6 µm are used, with short-wave rays (wavelength of about 1 µm) being of great importance.

For drying colloidal capillary-porous materials, which include most food products, the absorptive and transmissive properties (permeability) of the surface layers play an important role. There is an inverse relationship between these characteristics: if the surface layer of the material has high absorptive properties, its permeability is low, and vice versa.

To ensure more intensive heating and drying of the material, it is desirable that its surface layers have high transmissive properties, i.e., so that the zone of maximum temperature penetrates significantly into the interior of the material.

The efficiency of heating and drying by infrared rays depends on the intensity of radiant heat exchange between the radiation source and the irradiated material subject to drying.

For convenience of study, it is expedient to classify generators by:

the wavelength of maximum radiation, dependent on the temperature of the emitter, into: a) light (short-wave) emitters with T = 1773–2073 K (1500–1800°C) and above, in whose radiation spectrum, at a temperature exceeding 1773–2073 K, part of the energy falls within the visible range; the radiation maximum falls in the region λmax≤1.3 µm (approximately), and b) dark (long-wave) emitters, in whose radiation spectrum invisible infrared rays predominate (λmax>1.3 µm); at an emitter temperature below 673–623 K (400–350°C), rays of visible light are entirely absent.

The application of IR radiation in technological processes of the food industry is one of the ways not only to improve the quality of products, but also a method for intensifying the process. The effective use of the IR irradiation method should be based on an analysis of the process from the standpoint of modern physical theory of the effect of electromagnetic radiation on the object of heat treatment. For the heat treatment of various materials, radiation falling mainly within the infrared region of the spectrum is used.

During the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter, the temperature, moisture content, structure, and other properties of the irradiated material change. At the same time, the main characteristics of irradiation also change — the volumetric and surface density of the heat energy flow absorbed by the material. The optical properties of the material also change, in particular the material's absorptive capacity for IR rays. Experimental studies have shown that for different materials, the experimental optical characteristics are individual. Therefore, the effective use of IR heating in a technological process is achieved when the optical properties of the drying object are studied and matched with the spectral and energy characteristics of the IR generators.

The optical properties of plant-origin products are determined not only by the presence of water in them, but also by their own properties, as well as by the nature of the bond of moisture with the material. In plant-origin products, water is not an ordinary inert filler, but plays a structural-functional role in plant cells and in the biochemical processes continuously occurring in them.

## Laboratory Setup

The drying setup, developed by department staff members I. V. Dubkovetskyi and T. V. Burlaka, is a chamber 1 made of sheets of polished aluminum, which has a high IR ray reflection coefficient of 0.86 (for comparison, gold — 0.98; silver — 0.97; chrome surface — 0.72), which improves the conditions for creating uniformity of irradiation of the drying object. The air flow moves under the action of the blades of fan 2, passing sequentially through electric heater 3 and drying chamber 1, in which it contacts the material, supplying heat to it and removing evaporated moisture.

The material (raw material) subject to drying is beforehand prepared by washing, chopping, and forming a layer by spreading it on a special mesh support in box-shaped mesh basket 5, which is installed in the housing of drying chamber 1.

Inside the drying chamber, along vertical guides 17, the units of IR generators 4 can be moved and fixed in various positions, which makes it possible to change the degree of irradiation of the samples and the volume of the working chamber. The design of the IR emitter unit allows the use of both "light" and "dark" IR generators for drying products.

The setup provides for a combined radiation-convective method of drying food products. For this, heated air from the heater is supplied above the drying object at a certain velocity, whose value is regulated by speed variator 9 of electric fan 2.

The unit for registering the decrease in mass of the material being dried is a modified design of a photo-scale, made on the basis of analytical balance 7, connected via rod 6 to box-shaped mesh basket 5.

On control block 11, the temperature of the heat carrier and the range for switching the heating elements on and off are set, by means of which the irradiation time of the product is changed. Energy consumption per kilogram of finished product or kilogram of evaporated moisture is determined using meter 10. Air recirculation is regulated using damper 8. The change in relative humidity of the air at the inlet to and outlet from the drying chamber is recorded and regulated using regulator 13. The heater and IR generators can be de-energized with automatic switches 12. The change in temperature across the cross-section of the product is measured with a thermometer with thermocouples 15.

Drying is carried out in a pulsed heating–cooling regime. In this case, heating is carried out by IR rays to a specified moisture content, with a wavelength in the range of 1.2–4 µm and a flux density of 6–15 kW/m², for 3.0–110 s, until a limiting temperature in the chamber of 55–60°C is reached, and cooling is carried out for 9.0–330 s. The temperature is recorded by contact temperature sensor 16, whose signal is sent to relay 18 to switch the heating elements on or off. As soon as the drying temperature reaches the specified value, emitters 4 are switched off and the material begins to cool. When the material cools to a temperature value of 45–50°C, the emitters automatically switch on and the drying process continues analogously to that described above, until the material reaches the specified moisture content.

## Procedure

1. We warm up the setup for 10–15 minutes. For this, we set the temperature on the controller of control panel 11, open damper 8 to the specified degree, and sequentially turn on fan 2, heater 3, and IR generators 4.

2. We chop the material to be dried (potatoes, beets, carrots, etc.), with a mass of 100 g (50 g), into thin strips 2–5 mm thick.

3. We turn off heater 3, IR generators 4, and fan 2, remove basket 5 from chamber 1, load 100 g (50 g) of the material's strips into it, and fasten it in the drying chamber on rod 6; we tightly close the drying chamber.

4. We sequentially turn on fan 2 and heater 3 and immediately record the start time of drying.

5. We record the change in the material's mass during drying (from the dial of scale 7) and enter it into table 22.1. For this, during the first 15 minutes of drying, we record the scale readings in table 22.1 every 2 minutes, and thereafter — every 5 minutes, and so on until the scale readings stop changing, i.e., until the material reaches the equilibrium moisture content.

6. We sequentially turn off the power supply of heater 3, IR generators 4, and fan 2, remove basket 5 from the chamber, and inspect the state of the dried material.
