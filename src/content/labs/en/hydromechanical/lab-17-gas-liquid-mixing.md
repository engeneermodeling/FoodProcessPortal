# Laboratory Work №17. Power Consumption During Mechanical Mixing in a Gas–Liquid System

## Theoretical Background

The purpose of mixing a gas–liquid system with a dispersed gas phase is uniform distribution of the gas, intensification of heat and mass transfer processes, and chemical and biochemical reactions. Mechanical mixing is also used for defoaming.

The gas phase is supplied to the apparatus from below through a distribution device (sparger) and, due to the large density difference between the phases, moves upward, mixing the system. The intensity of pneumatic mixing is sufficient for the normal conduct of many technological processes.

Additional mechanical mixing is needed for significant intensification of mass transfer between gas and liquid. Such a need arises during microbiological synthesis, widespread in food and microbiological production, due to the high consumption rate of oxygen, which is poorly soluble in water. In this case, the rate of mass transfer of oxygen to the liquid directly depends on the power consumption of mixing.

Introducing gas into the liquid reduces the power consumption of mixing, because the density of the medium decreases

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image1.png" alt="formula" /> (17.1)</div>

where Vg and Vl are, respectively, the volumes of gas and liquid; ρg, ρl, ρgl are, respectively, the density of the gas, the liquid, and the gas–liquid mixture; φ = Vg/(Vg + Vl) is the gas content (a fairly close approximation, given the low density of the gas compared to the density of the liquid).

To calculate the rate of power consumption (power) during mixing of a gas–liquid system Ngl, one must know the relative mixing power, which, given the constancy of the Euler criterion according to equation (17.1), has the form

Ngl/Nl = 1–φ,	 (17.2)

where Nl is the power of mixing a homogeneous liquid, determined by the usual method.

However, equation (17.2) is not used due to the non-constancy of gas content. A distinction is made between local gas content φloc and its average value over the apparatus φavg, which for cylindrical apparatuses is determined by the equation

φavg  = (Hgl – Hl)/Hgl,	(17.3)

where Hl, Hgl are the height of the liquid layer and of the gas–liquid system in the apparatus.

Into equation (17.2), one should substitute the value of gas content in the mixing zone (local φloc), which can be determined only experimentally, by measuring it at various points of this local zone.

The relative power is a function of a number of criteria:

Ngl/Nl = f(Ka, We, Fr, Γ),	(17.4)

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image2.png" alt="formula" /> where Ka is the aeration criterion (gas flow), Ka = wsf/wag; We is the Weber criterion; Fr is the Froude criterion; Γ is the geometric similarity simplex; wsf is the superficial gas velocity, wsf = 4Qg/D²; wag is the velocity of the flows created by the agitator (for rotating agitators wag = πndag, for vibrating ones wag ≈ fa); σ is the surface tension; l is the defining linear dimension, usually taken as the agitator diameter dag; Qg is the gas flow rate; D is the apparatus diameter; n is the rotational speed of the agitator; f and a are the frequency and amplitude of oscillations during vibratory mixing.</div>

The main defining criterion in equation (17.4) is the aeration criterion Ka, with an increase of which the relative power falls, reaching a value of 0.2–0.3. In the simplest case, the relative mixing power can be determined using the equation

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image5.png" alt="formula" /> (17.6)</div>

where C, m are a constant and an exponent (dependent on the agitator's design).

The aeration criterion characterizes the interaction of the gas flow with the flows of the working medium created by the agitator. The medium's flows, exiting the agitator zone, deflect the gas flow, and it flows around the agitator, which reduces the gas content around it and increases the relative mixing power. This is accounted for by the aeration criterion.

At the same time, the liquid flows disperse the gas phase and increase the gas content. With a significant effect of this factor, accounted for by the Weber criterion, determining the relative power using only the aeration criterion becomes impossible.

## Laboratory Setup

The experimental setup (see fig. 17.1) is a cylindrical apparatus 1 with a diameter of 140 mm, made of glass and stainless steel. Inside the apparatus is placed a vibratory mixing device, consisting of rod 2 with removable perforated baffles 3 fastened to it. The rod is connected to an eccentric vibrator (not shown in the figure). In the lower part of the apparatus, sparger 4 is installed, connected to the compressed gas (air) supply line, and coil heat exchanger 5, connected to a thermostat. The gas flow rate is regulated by valves 7 and 8, measured with rotameter 9, and monitored by a gas meter. The design of the eccentric vibrator allows setting several fixed values of oscillation amplitude (from 1 to 6 mm).

<div class="lab-figure">
  <img src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image6.png" alt="Figure for laboratory work №17" loading="lazy" />
</div>

The vibrator's electric motor is powered by direct current from a selenium rectifier, connected to the AC mains through an autotransformer, which ensures smooth regulation of the oscillation frequency. The frequency is measured with a remote electric tachometer, whose sensor is fastened to the vibrator's shaft.

The mixing power is determined by the electrical method, from the difference between the power of the working stroke and the idle stroke (without liquid), accounting for losses on the motor's active resistance R = 19 Ω.

The apparatus is also equipped with drain valve 6 and nozzle 11 for filling with liquid and releasing gas.

To measure the concentration of dissolved oxygen in the apparatus, oxygen sensor 10 is provided.

## Procedure

1. We fill the apparatus with liquid (water), turn on the thermostat, and set the specified liquid temperature. We measure the liquid level Hl.

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image7.png" alt="formula" /> 2. We turn on the compressor, and using valves 7 and 8, set the required gas (air) flow rate according to rotameter 9. We turn on the vibrator and set the specified oscillation frequency f (the oscillation amplitude is set beforehand by turning the vibrator's eccentric). We measure the working mixing power, the current strength of the vibrator's motor Igl, as well as the height of the gas–liquid layer Hgl.</div>

3. We install gas content sensor 10 in the agitator's operating zone and measure the local gas content at various points of this zone.

4. We measure the quantities specified in points 2 and 3 at various oscillation frequencies and gas flow rates (according to the specified program).

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-17/image8.png" alt="formula" /> 5. We turn off the compressor and measure the working power and the current strength of the vibrator's motor Il during mixing of the liquid without gas supply, at all oscillation frequencies planned in the experiment.</div>

6. We turn off the thermostat, drain the water from the apparatus through drain valve 6, and measure the power Nx and current strength Ix at idle stroke, varying the oscillation parameters according to the specified program. It is recommended to measure the power and current strength at idle stroke twice: before the start of the experiments and after their completion. We turn off the vibrator.

We repeat all measurements at least three times and enter their average value into the experimental data table.

For comparison, it is recommended to carry out analogous work with ordinary mixing (rotating mixing devices), replacing the vibratory agitator with a turbine one and installing four baffles in the apparatus.
