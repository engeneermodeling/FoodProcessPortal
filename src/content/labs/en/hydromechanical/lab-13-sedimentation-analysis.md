# Laboratory Work №13. Conducting Sedimentation Analysis on a Tubular Supercentrifuge

## Theoretical Background

In various branches of the food industry, one often has to deal with a solid phase dispersed in a liquid. One of the most important properties of the disperse phase is its dispersity (particle size). On it depend not only the parameters of the technological process, but also the taste qualities, tendency to stratify, and transparency of food products. Because of this, there is often a need to determine the size and size distribution of particles both in the dry state and in suspensions.

There are various methods of dispersion analysis: sieve, microscopic, and sedimentation. The most accurate and complete analysis of dispersity, with comparatively small time expenditure, is given by the sedimentation method, which consists of experimentally determining the settling velocity of disperse phase particles dispersed in any dispersion medium.

Since the settling velocity of particles in the simplest case is proportional to the square of their size, by measuring it one can find the average size of suspension particles through direct observation. Therefore, sedimentation analysis is widely used both in scientific and in production practice. It can be carried out in the field of gravitational forces, or in centrifugal and other (for example, electric) force fields.

For studies of particle size distribution in finely dispersed systems, where a laminar regime of particle flow-around by the medium is realized, centrifugal sedimentation analysis is most often applied, which makes it possible to significantly accelerate the settling process. It is especially convenient to carry out this analysis on tubular supercentrifuges by measuring the concentration of the suspension's solid phase before and after centrifugation, as well as the mass of sediment in different bands along the height of the centrifuge drum.

## Laboratory Setup

The setup (fig. 13.5) consists of supercentrifuge 5, mounted on a frame, and three tanks: 16 — for pure dispersion medium (water), 14 — for the liquid drained from the centrifuge, and 10 — for the suspension under study. The tanks are connected to centrifugal pump 12, which supplies the suspension to the centrifuge drum. In tank 10, agitator 8 is installed for mixing, and thermometer 9 for measuring the temperature of the suspension. On the suction pipelines leading from each tank, there are valves 11, 13, 15.

The largest unit of the centrifuge is cast housing 5, in the upper part of which is a drive head with an elastic shaft installed in it, with the drum on the lower end. The elastic shaft is rotated by a belt drive from an electric motor. The lower end of the drum rotates in a textolite bearing (ring), installed so that the drum can self-align during operation. In the lower part of the centrifuge housing, a feed head with a replaceable nozzle is installed on a thread, connected by tube 6 to pump 12. On tube 6 there is valve 7 for regulating the amount of liquid supplied. To nozzle 3 is attached a glass tube leading to tank 14. This tube makes it possible to check the correctness of the centrifuge's start-up by the absence of liquid in it.

The liquid from the drum after centrifugation is discharged through chute 4 into glass cylinder 2 and enters tank 16, or tank 14, depending on the position of the handle of three-way valve 1.

For measuring and controlling the operation of the centrifuge, there is a main power switch for the setup; toggle switches for turning on the agitator, the centrifuge's electric motor, and the pump supplying the suspension; a rheostat for regulation, and a tachometer for measuring the rotational speed of the drum.

## Procedure

1. Before starting the work, we inspect the setup and make sure it is in working order.

2. We select the required clarifying drum, then disassemble it, clean it of contaminants, install the distribution rings inside, having first assembled them in order, after which we assemble the drum, screwing in the bottom with the trunnion (while screwing in, one must ensure a reliable fit of the paper gasket).

3. Having tightened the bottom with a special wrench, we lubricate the hollow trunnion with technical petroleum jelly (lubrication must be done each time before installing the drum).

4. With an open-end wrench, we screw in the feed head (left-hand thread) and check whether the nozzle is clogged, whether there are cracks on the textolite ring, and then put it in its place.

5. We disconnect the tachometer sensor wires, remove the protective shield of the belt drive and the belt. Having loosened the drive head's screw, we raise it 2–3 cm upward and fasten it with the same screw. Having checked the mating surfaces of the drum and shaft, we carefully install the drum in place. We put the chutes and cover in place, securely screw the shaft into the drum head, press the cover with springs, and screw in the drive head's screw. We put on the belt, install the protective shield, connect the wires to the tachometer sensor. We check that valves 7, 11, 13, and 15 are closed, and that three-way valve 1 is set so that the liquid returns to tank 16 (i.e., it must be turned to the left).

6. Into tank 16 we pour pure liquid (dispersion medium), and into tank 10 — suspension of a certain concentration.

7. We turn on the main power of the setup with the switch, and the agitator to mix the suspension. We measure the temperature of the suspension. After the suspension has been well mixed, we start the centrifuge. For this, we turn on the toggle switch of the centrifuge's electric motor and, by smoothly moving the rheostat slider upward, increase the rotational speed of the drum to the specified value. During this, we monitor the drum's rotational speed with the tachometer. When we reach about 9000 rpm, we move the rheostat slider upward to exceed the critical rotational speed for 1–2 s. After full excitation, the motor can run idle for 1–2 minutes, but during this one must make sure that the lubrication system of the drive head is working correctly.

8. When a constant rotational speed is established, we turn on the pump with the toggle switch and supply pure liquid, opening valve 15 and slowly opening valve 7. The start-up is considered correct if the liquid does not flow back from nozzle 3.

9. After the centrifuge has operated for about 1 minute, we close valve 15, and turn valve 1 to the right (to drain the liquid into tank 14).

10. We note the level of suspension in tank 10, simultaneously start the stopwatch, and open valve 11. During this, we record the rotational speed of the drum. After the specified volume of suspension has been centrifuged, we turn off pump 12 supplying suspension from tank 10, take the second reading of time and volume of suspension in tank 10, and turn off suspension agitator 8. After this, we stop the centrifuge by moving the rheostat slider down, and turn off all toggle switches.

11. Having stopped the centrifuge and drained the remaining liquid, we remove the drum, place it on a special table, unscrew the lower cover, carefully remove the rings with sediment, weigh them in turn — first with sediment, then without sediment — and record in the table the mass of wet sediment on each ring (the moisture content of the sediment can be determined by drying, or taken conditionally).

12. After finishing the work, we wash the centrifuge and wipe it dry, lightly lubricating the metal parts with liquid oil. We also wash the drum, wipe it dry, lightly lubricate it with liquid oil, and put it in storage.

We proceed to processing the experimental results.
