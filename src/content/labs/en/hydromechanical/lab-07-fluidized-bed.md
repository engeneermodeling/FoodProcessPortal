# Laboratory Work №7. Study of the Hydrodynamics of a Fluidized Bed

## Theoretical Background

Granular material, consisting of individual solid particles (grains) of similar size, most often has to be processed in a gas or liquid medium. Such thermal or diffusional processing of granular material is expediently carried out in the so-called fluidized (suspended) state, which forms at certain supply velocities of the moving continuous medium.

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-07/image1.png" alt="formula" /> In an air stream, a suspended bed of granular material forms when using roughly spherical particles of similar size, with an equivalent diameter ranging from hundredths of a millimeter (flour, starch) to several centimeters (dragée, soybeans, walnuts). In order for all particles of the material to transition into the suspended state simultaneously, without forming separate stagnation zones or intense fountaining, it is necessary that the mass of individual particles not differ by more than an order of magnitude, i.e., that the linear dimension (equivalent diameter) of the particles, at equal density, not differ by more than a factor of 2.14.</div>

In the fluidized state, various products are dried (granulated sugar, cotton, grain, sawdust), fine particles are granulated into larger ones, and particles of granular material are coated with decorative, protective, or stimulating shells.

A fluidized bed, i.e., a bed of solid particles suspended in a gas stream, is obtained by blowing air from bottom to top through a bed of granular material with fairly similarly sized particles, placed on a grate. Upon reaching a certain air velocity, the bed expands, and the particles within its boundaries begin chaotic motion, which outwardly resembles a boiling process. For this reason, such a bed is also called boiling, or suspended.

Several states of the fluidized bed are observed:

initial calm fluidization with an insignificant shift of the material's particles relative to their initial position, when there is no noticeable effect of the gas on their mobility (the volume of such a bed increases somewhat compared to the fixed bed);

transitional regime — the bed's "boiling up", i.e., the transition of particles into chaotic motion;

full fluidization with intensive chaotic motion of particles — vortex boiling.

If the density of air is neglected compared to the density of the solid particle material, equation (7.1) takes the form

R = G. 	(7.6)

When all particles of the bed transition into the suspended state, the gas pressure from below the bed ΔPb must balance the weight of the particles per unit cross-sectional area f of the apparatus:

ΔPb = Gb/f ,	(7.7)

where Gb is the weight of all particles in the bed, N.

From equation (7.7) it follows that during fluidization, the hydraulic resistance of the bed ΔPb does not depend on air velocity or the height (volume) of the bed, and remains practically constant over the entire range of the bed's existence. The constancy of ΔPb is explained by the fact that as the air flow rate and its superficial velocity wsf increase, the volume of the suspended bed and the distance between particles increase simultaneously, but the actual air velocity between particles, on which the bed's resistance depends, does not change.

By the superficial air velocity wsf is meant its volumetric flow rate referred to the full cross-sectional area f of the empty apparatus:

wsf = V/f. 	(7.8)

The actual air velocity w in the gaps between particles, which is rather difficult to measure, always exceeds the superficial velocity.

The state of the suspended bed is approximately characterized by porosity ε, by which is meant the volumetric fraction of gas-filled voids in the bed:

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-07/image9.png" alt="formula" /> For a fixed bed of spherical particles of equal diameter, the voidage (porosity) ε is approximately equal to 0.4, regardless of their diameter. For a suspended bed, porosity ε increases with increasing gas flow rate, since the total bed volume Vb increases while the particle volume remains unchanged. At the entrainment velocity wen (the limiting velocity for the suspended bed), it can be considered that Vb≈Vs and ε = 1. Therefore, a suspended bed of spherical particles of equal diameter can exist within a porosity range from ε = 0.4 (at wsf = wcr) to ε = 1 (at wsf = wen). With some approximation, these limits can also be adopted for rounded particles of irregular or polyhedral shape.</div>

If the hydraulic resistance ΔPb of granular bed 5 in the apparatus is measured at various values of superficial air velocity wsf using U-shaped differential manometer 8 (fig. 7.2), we obtain the curve ΔPb = f(wsf), called the fluidization curve.

Fig. 7.3, a shows the ideal fluidization curve, characteristic of monodisperse, i.e., equally sized, spherical particles. The rising portion of the curve 0A corresponds to air movement through the fixed bed, and its shape depends on the air flow regime. For small particles, the flow regime is laminar and segment 0A is straight. For larger particles, according to the laws of the transitional or turbulent air flow regime, segment 0A is curvilinear.

<div class="lab-formula"><img class="lab-formula-img" src="/images/labs/hydromechanical/lab-07/image10.png" alt="formula" /></div>

The break in the curve at point A corresponds to the transition of the fixed bed into the calm fluidized state at the critical air velocity wcr, when the hydrodynamic pressure force of the gas stream balances the weight of the bed particles (accounting for the Archimedean force). With further increase in gas velocity, the pressure drop in the bed remains constant (horizontal segment AB).

At point B, the gas velocity, called the entrainment velocity wen, reaches such a high value that particles are carried out of the bed (pneumatic transport of particles begins). As a result of the entrainment of a certain amount of particles, the total weight of the remaining particles and the energy required to keep them suspended decrease, i.e., the pressure drop ΔPb decreases.

Fig. 7.3, b shows the real fluidization curve for monodisperse non-spherical particles. On segment 0A, the bed is in the fixed state, and its resistance increases with increasing air velocity.

As the air velocity approaches the critical value, the lifting force of the flow acting on the grains (particles) increases so much that their mutual pressure disappears. Due to the irregular shape of the grains, the point of application of the lifting force mostly does not coincide with the center of gravity of the grains. The resulting torque slightly shifts the grains and orients them in the direction of the air jets. The granular bed swells (segment AB), increasing in volume by 3–5%, depending on the shape of the grains.

At point B, when the resistance of the swollen bed reaches its maximum value, a reorientation of all grains occurs, after which the bed's resistance drops rather sharply by an insignificant amount (section BC). The decrease in resistance is explained by some increase in the cross-section of the channels through which air passes between the grains. The air velocity at point C is considered critical. It corresponds to the beginning of fluidization, when the particles are for the most part still poorly mobile, but local pockets of pseudo-boiling (particle motion) arise in individual places in the bed.

A gradual increase in air velocity above wcr promotes the emergence of ever more boiling pockets (transitional regime), the intensity of grain motion in the bed increases, and boiling becomes more uniform. The bed's resistance increases somewhat (segment CD), and then, from a certain boiling velocity wboil, it practically remains constant (segment DE).

The boiling velocity wboil characterizes the attainment of full fluidization of the bed — a state of vortex boiling, in which the material's grains are in an unsteady, intensively chaotic-cyclic motion. In carrying out technological processes, it is considered that full fluidization (boiling) sets in when the height of the expanded bed of intensively moving particles exceeds the height of the fixed particle bed by two to three times. In the boiling state, the fullest contact between solid particles and the gas stream is achieved.

On the real fluidization curve, the forward and reverse path lines, obtained respectively with a gradual increase and gradual decrease in gas velocity, do not coincide near point C (hysteresis). The reverse path line (dashed) has no section of pressure increase and is located lower. The reverse path line is well reproduced in all experiments, whereas the forward path line coincides less well in different experiments and may take the form of a broken line, especially if channels form in the granular bed.

Depending on the design of the apparatus and the gas distribution grate, various bed motion regimes can arise: fountaining or particle movement along a contour (vortex regime), the presence of several circulation contours, the formation of gas bubbles (slugging regime). Uniform fluidization is achieved rather rarely: if the gas distributor has the form of a porous partition, or in the case of fluidized bed formation in a liquid medium.

## Laboratory Setup

The main element of the setup is glass column 6 (see fig. 7.2). Above the gas-distribution section 3 of the column, distribution grate 4 is installed, on which bed 5 of wheat grains rests (de from 3.7 to 4.1 · 10⁻³ m). In the upper part of the column, catching mesh 7 is fastened to retain the granular material in case of possible entrainment.

Air is supplied into the column by fan 1 through orifice plate 10 and is regulated by valve 2. The air flow rate is determined from the calibration graph attached to differential manometer 9, based on the pressure drop readings at the orifice plate. Knowing the air flow rate and the internal diameter of the column, the air velocity wsf is found. Differential manometer 8 serves to determine the hydraulic resistance of the bed ΔP at various air velocities.

## Procedure

1. Before starting the work, check that valve 2 is closed. After this, turn on the fan and gradually open valve 2, setting such an air flow rate into the column that the reading of differential manometer 8 does not exceed 100 Pa (10 mm water column). Then increase the air flow rate so that, until fluidization begins, the bed's resistance (pressure drop in the differential manometer) increases by no more than 30–50 Pa. Gradually increase the air flow rate until the onset of full fluidization with intensive mixing (vortex boiling), without allowing particles to be thrown up to the catching mesh.

2. In each experiment, measure the height Hb and the hydraulic resistance ΔPb of the bed, the hydraulic resistance of the orifice plate ΔPo, and record the characteristic of the bed's state (fixed, shifted, mobile, fluidized).
