# Laboratory Work №20. Evaporation in a Double-Effect Evaporator

## Main Principles

Evaporation is the process of concentrating solutions of non-volatile substances by removing the solvent in the form of vapor during boiling. The evaporation process is characterized by the fact that during the boiling of the solution, only the pure solvent passes into vapor. In industry, aqueous solutions of various substances are most often evaporated, i.e., water is removed in the form of water vapor, called secondary vapor.

Most often, only part of the solvent is removed from the solution, since in evaporators traditionally used in the food industry, the concentrated solution remains in the liquid state. Evaporation is carried out under atmospheric pressure or under reduced pressure. In the first case, the secondary vapor is discharged into the atmosphere; in the second — into a condenser, where a vacuum is created in the apparatus as a result of the condensation of the secondary vapor.

The vacuum in the system is maintained by removing non-condensed gases, mainly air, with a vacuum pump. Evaporation under vacuum makes it possible to lower the boiling point of the solution, as well as to increase the useful temperature difference (between the heating vapor and the boiling solution), which contributes to a reduction of the heat-transfer surface.

In the food industry, multiple-effect (two to six apparatuses connected in series) and single-effect evaporator setups are used. In co-current multiple-effect evaporators, the secondary vapor formed in the preceding effect is used as heating vapor for the next one, for which purpose the pressure is reduced successively from effect to effect of the setup. This scheme provides a significant reduction in the amount of primary (heating) vapor supplied to the first effect, and thereby makes it possible to significantly improve the technical and economic performance of the entire evaporator setup.

## Laboratory Setup

The laboratory setup (see fig. 20.1) consists of evaporators 2 and 3, condenser 5, and control and measuring instruments: thermometers 8 and measuring cylinders 9, 10.

Using heater 1, the aqueous solution in the first effect 2 of the evaporator setup is first heated, and then evaporated. The vapor formed enters coil 4, where, as a result of condensation, it heats and maintains in a boiling state the liquid in the second effect 3.

The vapor formed in the second effect condenses in coil 7 of condenser 5.

The amount of secondary vapor from the first and second effects equals the amount of condensate in collectors 9 and 10. Funnels 6 are used to fill the effects of the evaporator setup.

## Procedure

1. We fill effects 2 and 3 to 2/3 with solution and low-boiling liquid, respectively, recording their volumes, initial concentrations, and temperatures in table 20.1.

2. We turn on heater 1 of the first effect and record the time of switching on. When the first droplets of condensate appear in collector 9, we record the time these droplets appear, as well as droplets of condensate in collector 10. The appearance of condensate signifies the onset of boiling of the liquids in the effects.

3. When condensate appears in collector 9, we supply cooling water to condenser 5, and after condensate appears in collector 10, we record the temperature of the liquid in the second effect, the mass of condensate in collector 9, and take a sample of the solution from the first effect.

4. After approximately 1/5 of the liquid in the second effect has evaporated, we end the experiment by turning off the heater of the first effect and the water supply to condenser 5. After this, we take a sample of the solution from the first effect.

We record the experimental and calculated data in table 20.1.

## Processing the Results

1. From temperatures t1in, t1int, t1fin, using a reference source, we find the concentrations of the solution — initial B1in, at the moment the liquid in the second effect begins to boil B2in, and at the end of the experiment B1fin. We compare the found concentrations with those determined by sample analysis. We explain the presence of a difference.

2. From the dry substance balance in the first effect

S1B1in = (S1 – W1int)B2in

we find the mass of water evaporated in the first effect during the heating time of the liquid in the second effect from t2in to t2fin. We compare the calculated W1int with the practically obtained value, and explain the difference.

3. From the dry substance balance in the first effect

S1B1in = (S1 – W1fin)B2fin

we find the mass of water evaporated in the first effect during the entire experiment. We compare the calculated and experimental values of W1fin, and explain the difference.

4. From the heat balance for the period of heating the liquid in the second effect from t2in to t2fin

W1int·i1 + S2c2t2in = W1int·cctc + S2c2t2fin

we find the heat flow transferred from the secondary vapor of the first effect to the liquid being heated in the second effect.

In the balance equation, the condensate temperature tc can be taken as 95°C.
